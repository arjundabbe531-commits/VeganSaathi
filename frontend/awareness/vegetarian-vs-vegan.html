Vegetarian vs Vegan — Not the Same Thing

Comparing dietary categories in India: Lacto-Vegetarian, Eggetarian, Jain, and Vegan dietary principles.

In everyday conversation across India, words like "veg", "pure veg", and "vegan" are frequently conflated. However, nutritional science, ethical frameworks, and religious traditions distinguish these patterns clearly. Understanding each definition is vital when ordering food, eating at college canteens, or using VeganSaathi's search filters.

Comparative Dietary Overview

Dietary Category What It Excludes What It Includes Indian Cultural Context
Lacto-Vegetarian ("Pure Veg") Meat, poultry, fish, seafood, eggs Dairy (milk, ghee, curd, paneer), grains, pulses, all vegetables, fruits India’s cultural and commercial default for "Vegetarian" / green dot label.
Eggetarian Meat, poultry, fish, seafood Eggs, dairy products, grains, pulses, vegetables, fruits Common collegiate practice where eggs are consumed for convenience or protein, but meat is avoided.
Jain Diet Meat, fish, eggs, root vegetables (onion, garlic, potato, carrots), honey Dairy products, above-ground vegetables, pulses, grains, fruits, nuts Grounded in the spiritual principle of Ahimsa (non-violence) toward plants and soil organisms.
Vegan All animal-derived products: meat, fish, eggs, dairy, honey, animal fats 100% plant-derived foods: pulses, grains, vegetables (including root vegetables), fruits, nuts, seeds Excludes all dairy regardless of cultural sanctification.
Plant-Based (General) Minimizes animal products to varying degrees Emphasizes whole plant foods (vegetables, grains, legumes, nuts) Often adopted primarily for cardiovascular health or climate impact.

Why "Pure Veg" Does Not Mean "Vegan-Safe" in India

In western countries, vegetarian food often implies eggs or cheese. In India, however, "pure vegetarian" restaurants exclude eggs completely, but place dairy at the very heart of their kitchen:

  • Cooking mediums: Dal tadka, biryanis, and sabzis are frequently tempered in desi ghee or butter.
  • Gravy bases: North Indian curries often blend fresh cream (malai), curd (dahi), or cashew-milk paste.
  • Rotis & Naans: Chapatis and parathas are brushed with ghee or butter unless requested otherwise.
  • Sweets & Desserts: Traditional sweets (gulab jamun, peda, barfi, jalebi) are built on milk solids (khoya/mawa) or fried in ghee.

Consequently, a restaurant bearing a prominent green dot or "Pure Veg" sign is rarely vegan by default. A diner must specifically ask whether items are prepared in vegetable oil without butter, ghee, or cream.

Jain Diet: A Distinct Philosophical Tradition

People unfamiliar with Indian traditions sometimes assume that Jain food is simply a stricter form of veganism. In reality, they represent two entirely different ethical paradigms:

Jain Food is NOT Automatically Vegan

Traditional Jain cuisine permits dairy products (milk, curd, ghee). Many beloved Jain dishes heavily incorporate paneer and ghee-tempered pulses.

Vegan Food is NOT Automatically Jain

Vegan food avoids animal products, but freely includes root vegetables like potatoes, onions, garlic, ginger, and carrots, which Jain practice avoids to protect underground life.
