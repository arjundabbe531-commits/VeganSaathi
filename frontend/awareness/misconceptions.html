Common Misconceptions About Veganism

Examining evidence, nutritional facts, and practical realities around plant-based living in India.

Myths and exaggerations exist on both sides of the vegan conversation. Some assume plant-based living is nutritionally impossible for students, while others claim a vegan diet automatically cures every illness. Let us examine the most frequent misconceptions with scientific accuracy and common sense.

Myth 1: "You cannot get enough protein without meat, eggs, or paneer."

The Evidence: Eating enough total calories from a varied plant-based diet, including pulses, soya, nuts and seeds, meets protein needs for most adults.

In an Indian context, pulses (toor, moong, masoor, chana, rajma), soya chunks, peanuts, sesame seeds, and millets (jowar, bajra) provide substantial protein. Traditional combinations—such as dal-rice or roti with chana—naturally provide complementary amino acids across the day without requiring complex food-combining rules at every individual meal.

Myth 2: "Vegan food is expensive, imported, and consists only of salads."

The Evidence: Western media often highlights expensive vegan specialty products—such as almond milk, imported vegan cheeses, and branded mock-meats. In India, however, the most affordable, foundational foods are already plant-based.

A student eating dal tadka, jeera rice, aloo bhaji, chapatis, idli-sambar, and roasted peanuts is eating a 100% plant-based meal. Fresh seasonal vegetables and local pulses at the Shegaon market are substantially cheaper per gram of nutrient than restaurant paneer dishes or packaged snacks.

Myth 3: "A vegan diet is automatically healthy (or automatically deficient)."

The Evidence: The healthfulness of any diet depends on what you actually eat, not merely what you exclude.

  • French fries, sugary sodas, refined biscuits, and deep-fried samosas cooked in vegetable oil can be strictly vegan, but eating only these items will not support vitality or athletic performance.
  • Conversely, Harvard Health reports that vegetarian diets can help reduce the chances of developing heart disease.
  • Crucial Vitamin B12 Fact: Vitamin B12 is produced by micro-organisms, not plants. Unfortified plant diets contain no reliable B12. The NHS advises that vegans use fortified foods or a supplement, because sources for vegans are limited.

Myth 4: "You have to be 100% perfect from day one, or it doesn't count."

The Evidence: The Vegan Society’s official definition explicitly qualifies veganism as excluding animal exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable."

College life in a hostel or campus mess presents real constraints. If you replace dairy milk with black coffee or tea, choose peanut chutney instead of butter, or eat plant-based four days a week, you are making meaningful, conscious progress. Avoid self-criticism or dogmatism.

Myth 5: "The FSSAI green dot means a packaged food is vegan."

The Evidence: In India, the familiar green dot indicates lacto-vegetarian status under FSSAI regulations—confirming only that the item is free of meat, fish, and eggs.

Green-dotted biscuits, chips, chocolates, and instant noodles routinely contain milk solids, whey powder, butter, or milk fat. To verify if a packaged product is vegan, always scan the ingredients list for milk derivatives.
