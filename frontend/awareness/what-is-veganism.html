What is Veganism?

Understanding the philosophy, dietary definitions, and what it looks like in an everyday Indian context.

The Definition of Veganism

Veganism is officially defined by The Vegan Society (founded in November 1944) as:

"A philosophy and way of living which seeks to exclude—as far as is possible and practicable—all forms of exploitation of, and cruelty to, animals for food, clothing or any other purpose; and by extension, promotes the development and use of animal-free alternatives for the benefit of animals, humans and the environment. In dietary terms it denotes the practice of dispensing with all products derived wholly or partly from animals."

Notice the key phrase: "as far as is possible and practicable." Veganism is not about rigid moral purity or unattainable perfection. It is a compassionate framework aimed at reducing harm to sentient animals wherever practical everyday alternatives exist.

Ethical Veganism vs. Plant-Based Diet

While the two terms are often used interchangeably in casual conversation, health researchers and philosophers draw an intentional distinction:

  • Ethical Veganism: A comprehensive lifestyle choice that avoids animal exploitation across all domains—including food, leather, wool, silk, cosmetics tested on animals, and entertainment involving captive animals.
  • Plant-Based Diet: A nutritional pattern focused predominantly or exclusively on foods derived from plants (grains, legumes, vegetables, fruits, nuts, seeds). Many people choose plant-based diets for health reasons. Harvard Health Publishing notes that vegetarian diets can help reduce the chances of developing heart disease.

What Vegan Dietary Choices Exclude & Include

Foods Excluded

  • Meat, poultry, fish, shellfish
  • Eggs and egg derivatives
  • Dairy products (milk, curd/dahi, paneer, butter, ghee, cheese, khoya, malai)
  • Honey and royal jelly
  • Animal by-products (gelatin, animal rennet, bone char refined sugar)

Foods Included

  • Legumes, dals, chana, rajma, sprouts
  • Grains, millets (jowar, bajra, ragi), rice, whole wheat
  • Vegetables, leafy greens, tubers, roots
  • Fruits, berries, melons
  • Nuts, peanuts, seeds (sesame/til, flax, sunflower)
  • Soy products (tofu, soya chunks, soy milk)

Why This Matters in the Indian Context

India is internationally renowned for having the largest vegetarian population on earth. However, traditional Indian vegetarian cuisine is predominantly lacto-vegetarian. Dairy products like ghee, butter, curd, buttermilk (chaas), and paneer are deeply woven into everyday culinary traditions and religious offerings.

Because of this, a sign reading "Pure Veg" in an Indian canteen or restaurant indicates the absence of meat and eggs, but almost always relies on dairy cooking mediums.
